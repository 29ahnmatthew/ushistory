Impact & Change

Economic, social and geographic consequences

Economic Change

Trade increased, factories also grew, and more Americans started making goods to sell instead of making everything for themselves.

Erie Canal opened in 1825 and lowered shipping cost between the NYC to Great Lakes.

Social Change

Many people moved to the cities to work in the factory. Women also gained new job opportunities, but working condition was different with men.

The Lowell textile mills in Massachusetts employed a lot of young women/girl during the 1820s to 1840.

Political and Geographic Change

The government invested in transportation, to improve trade and connect the nation.

The national road was funded by the federal government, and NY stated funded Erie Canal.

Unintended Environmental Effects

Building canals, roads, and railroads changed natural landscape and enviornment.

Before & After: What Changed?

Qualitative comparison. These describe broad trends rather than claiming every American experienced the same change.

Before / Earlier patterns

Local production & trade

Many rural households produced some of their own goods and sold largely in nearby markets. Poor transport limited distant trade.

After / Expanding patterns

Regional & national markets

Canals, roads and railroads expanded market connections; larger enterprises and wage work became more important in many places.

Before / Earlier patterns

Artisan apprenticeship

Skilled trades often trained workers through long apprenticeships, as an 1836 blacksmith contract documents.

After / Expanding patterns

Factory labor

Textile mills brought workers into more centralized, wage-based production, without eliminating older crafts overnight.

Source starting points: American Yawp, ch. 8; 1836 apprenticeship contract.
